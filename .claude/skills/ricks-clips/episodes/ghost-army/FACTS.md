# THE GHOST ARMY — facts and sources (checked 2026-11-17)

Rule (Rick pattern, finding 10): the narration carries no real names and no dates; the facts live here and in the set details (patches, stencils, period props).

## Facts used in the script
| # | Fact | Confidence | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | A secret US Army unit, the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops, about 1,100 men, activated 20 January 1944; most of it reached England in May 1944. First mobile, multimedia tactical deception unit in US Army history. | high | ghostarmy.org; National WWII Museum; History.com |
| F2 | Many of its men were artists, designers, architects and art students, recruited partly through art schools (Pratt Institute, Cooper Union). Several became famous later (designer Bill Blass, painter Ellsworth Kelly, photographer Art Kane). | high | National WWII Museum; Hyperallergic; Smithsonian |
| F3 | Inflatable tanks: a frame of air tubes under a rubberized canvas skin, made by companies such as U.S. Rubber and Goodyear. Fake up close; from about a quarter mile (≈400 m) it looked like an M4 Sherman. A bulldozer drew fake track marks so aerial photos would not give it away. | high | Live Science; National WWII Museum; Task & Purpose |
| F4 | Anecdote (Arthur Shilstone, a Ghost Army soldier): two French civilians on bicycles saw four GIs lift a "40-ton" tank; a GI said "The Americans are very strong." Details vary between tellings (Normandy, weeks after D-Day). | medium (veteran's recollection) | Smithsonian; The Week; 99% Invisible |
| F5 | Sonic deception: sounds of tanks, trucks, bridge building and troops were recorded at Fort Knox on 16-inch transcription discs and played back from very heavy speakers mounted on M3 half-tracks; reported audible up to about 15 miles (≈24 km). | high (range: reported) | Task & Purpose; Hackaday; National WWII Museum |
| F6 | Radio deception: operators imitated the Morse "fist" (the personal sending rhythm, "like a fingerprint") of real units' operators. | high | History.com; 99% Invisible |
| F7 | "Special effects": fake shoulder patches, stenciled fake unit numbers on trucks, and staged talk in cafés and bars for German agents. | high | History.com; National WWII Museum |
| F8 | About 20-22 deception operations, June 1944 to March 1945. Operation Brest (Aug 1944): first mission combining visual, radio and sonic deception. | high (count varies) | ghostarmy.org; Live Science |
| F9 | Operation Viersen (March 1945): the largest. About 600 inflatables placed about 10 miles (≈16 km) south of the real Ninth Army Rhine crossing; the Germans aimed at the decoys while the real army crossed with little resistance. | high (dummy count reported) | History.com; Military History sources |
| F10 | Only 3 men of the unit were killed and several dozen wounded. | high | History.com / WUNC |
| F11 | The unit stayed secret for decades and was declassified in 1996 (about 50 years). Congressional Gold Medal 2022, ceremony 2024. | high | History.com; WUNC; Live Science |

## NOT used (unverified or wrong in popular retellings)
- "Saved 15,000-30,000 lives": no reliable primary source.
- "Impersonated generals": sources say they impersonated other units' soldiers, not generals specifically.
- "Simulated 40,000 troops": unverified.
- A Sherman weighs about 30-33 t; the anecdote's "40 tons" is the soldier's figure. The narration says "thirty-ton" (accurate) and keeps "Americans are very strong" as the soldier's joke.
